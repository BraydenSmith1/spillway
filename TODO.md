## 2. Known limitations — put these in any writeup

1. **~14 SCED days permanently missing** (~1.3%). Most were never published by
   ERCOT: no archive exists at the posting date. A few are oversized archives
   (2024-08-05 is ~220 MB) that break mid-transfer. Not recoverable by retrying.
2. **Load forecasts only go back to 2026-03-26.** ERCOT retains ~6 months.
   These are therefore *entirely post-RTC* and cannot support before/after work.
3. **ESR (battery) data starts 2025-12-05**, the RTC+B go-live. It does not
   exist before that — batteries got their own disclosure with the redesign.
4. **RTC+B (2025-12-05) is a structural break inside the window.** Do not train
   a model across it without accounting for the regime change.
5. **`lmp_resource_nodes` months before 2025-09** are missing the last day of
   each month (a bug fixed 2026-09-11). They are outside the analysis window;
   don't mix them in without re-fetching.
6. **`gridstatus` 0.36.0 cannot parse ERCOT's AS offer files after 2026-06-25.**
   `download_sced.py` patches around it. Revisit when upstream fixes it.
7. **`dam_settlement_point_prices` is missing 2026-03-07** — a single day, and
   notably the same date is missing from `sced_60day`. Worth one look before
   assuming it's a fetch failure rather than an ERCOT publication gap.
8. **SCED data ends ~64 days before today** and always will: the 60-day
   disclosure lag is deliberate. Anything needing generator-level data is
   structurally two months behind. Price and congestion data are current.

## TIER 0 — Measure the two numbers the business case rests on

> §4.10 says three variables break the model: curtailment rate, curtailment
> hours, and hashprice. You can't control hashprice. **You can now measure the
> other two.** Everything in §4 is arithmetic on assumptions until you do this.
> §18 lists it as action #1.

### 0.1 Build the curtailment calculation
- Read `data/raw/sced_60day/*.parquet`. Core columns: `SCED Timestamp`,
  `Resource Name`, `Resource Type`, `Telemetered Resource Status`, `HSL`,
  `LSL`, `Base Point`, `Telemetered Net Output`.
- Curtailment ≈ `HSL − Base Point`, but only where the unit was actually
  available. **Gotchas that decide whether this is credible:**
  - Exclude rows where `Telemetered Resource Status` is `OUT`/`ONTEST` — an
    offline unit is not curtailed.
  - Filter to `Resource Type` in `{WIND, PVGR}` for the renewable thesis.
  - Clip negatives to zero; `Base Point > HSL` happens and is noise.
  - Sanity-check against `Telemetered Net Output` — if actual output tracks
    `Base Point`, the dispatch instruction was followed and the gap is real.
- **Done when:** you can produce curtailed MWh per resource per day, and spot
  check one big event by hand against the raw 5-minute rows.

### 0.2 Split economic vs. ordered curtailment
- Join to `settlement_point_prices` on node + timestamp.
- Curtailment while price < $0 is largely *economic* — the generator declining
  to pay to produce. Curtailment at healthy prices is *ordered* (congestion).
- **This distinction is the thesis.** Your §1 claim is that spilled energy is
  worth ~$0 to its owner. That is true for ordered curtailment. It is much
  weaker for economic curtailment, where the generator chose not to run.
- **Done when:** you can state what share of spill at a node is each type.

### 0.3 ⚠️ The PTC correction
- For **PTC-earning wind**, curtailed energy is *not* worth $0 — stopping
  forfeits the production tax credit, so their reservation price is **negative**.
  This is why they bid negative and why 5% of intervals clear below zero.
- Your $5–15/MWh acquisition price (§4.2) holds best for **solar** (ITC is
  investment-based, so spilled MWh really are worth ~$0) and for post-PTC wind.
- **Action:** make fuel type and PTC status a hard screening criterion, not a
  footnote. §4.1 already picks solar — make that explicit and defensible.
- **Done when:** the target list excludes PTC-live wind, or prices it separately.

### 0.4 Replace the assumptions
- Compute per node: **curtailment rate %** (spilled ÷ potential), **hours/year**
  with meaningful spill, **average MW during events**, and the seasonal/diurnal
  shape.
- Compare against the §4.1 archetype: 26% capacity factor, 12% curtailment,
  1,800 hours.
- **Done when:** §4's P&L is rebuilt on measured numbers. If real curtailment
  is 6% and 900 hours, site-one economics halve — **you need to know that before
  pitching anyone**, not after.

## TIER 1 — The node screening model (§18 #4)

### 1.1 Build the crosswalk (`crosswalk/` is still empty)
Map `settlement point → resource name → project → county → nameplate MW →
fuel → owner → COD`. Sources: ERCOT's resource-node mapping, plus EIA-860 for
ownership, nameplate, and commercial operation date.
Without this, every finding is about `BATCAVE_RN` instead of a company you can
call. **This is the unglamorous step that makes the analysis actionable.**

### 1.2 Rank nodes
Score = measured curtailment GWh **×** capital-structure tractability (§6.3:
merchant, unlevered, single-asset, independently owned rank highest).

### 1.3 Produce the target list
20–50 **named** solar farms, each with its own measured spill.
This is what makes §13's call #3 work — you open with their asset's number.
**Done when:** you could send ten emails tomorrow, each with a real figure.

## TIER 2 — Congestion vs. oversupply, and whether it persists

### 2.1 Attribute curtailment to constraints
Join `shadow_prices` to curtailment events by timestamp. §10 correctly calls
this "the actual congestion-curtailment signal" — it tells you *which* line
bound and what it cost.

### 2.2 Separate structural from episodic ⭐
You are underwriting a **5–10 year exclusive** (§3.3). A node curtailed because
a line is chronically undersized is an annuity. A node curtailed by a one-off
outage is not.
- Test persistence: does the same constraint bind across months and seasons?
- Cross-check `resource_outages` — was the congestion just someone's maintenance?
- **This is the difference between a contract and a bad bet, and nothing else
  in your dataset answers it.**

### 2.3 Check the battery threat (§15 cannibalization risk)
Using `sced_esr`: are batteries already absorbing the spill at your target
nodes? Are they charging during curtailment hours? This is currently listed in
your risk register as unquantified — **you can now quantify it**, at least
post-RTC. If storage is already eating the curtailment at the best nodes, that
changes site selection fundamentally.

## TIER 3 — The forecast product (Layer 1, §3.1)

### 3.1 Baseline first
Predict tomorrow = today. **Any model must beat this.** Most don't. Skipping
this step is the most common way to fool yourself.

### 3.2 Features
Load forecast; wind/solar forecast **and forecast error**; outages; historical
congestion at the node; hour/season; DA/RT spread; recent curtailment.

### 3.3 Model
LightGBM **quantile regression → P10/P50/P90** (§10). Probabilistic output is
what makes a forecast contractable; a point estimate isn't sellable.

### 3.4 Evaluate honestly
MAE at 24h and 48h **by node** (§14), against the baseline, on a held-out
period. Respect the RTC break when splitting train/test.

### 3.5 Defer PyPSA
A DC power flow model underneath is real differentiation eventually. It is
worthless until 3.1–3.4 exist. **Don't start here** — it's the most
intellectually appealing and least valuable next step.
